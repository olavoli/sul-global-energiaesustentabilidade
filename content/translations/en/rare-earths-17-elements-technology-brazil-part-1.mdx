<Callout title="Key insight">

Holding underground reserves does not automatically translate into industrial or economic sovereignty. The real added value and the barrier to entry in the production chain lie in mastering chemical separation, refining, and the manufacture of alloys and components.

</Callout>

From electric motors to wind turbines, a particular group of chemical elements has become a strategic pillar of contemporary industry. Although Brazil holds some of the planet's largest estimated reserves, turning this mineral wealth into technological development requires overcoming complex scientific, industrial, and environmental obstacles. This analysis examines the nature of these materials, their critical applications, and the geological and geopolitical challenges shaping the current landscape.

## The invisible technological revolution

When an electric vehicle accelerates, a wind turbine converts wind into electricity, or a medical instrument performs a high-precision measurement, we rarely associate these events with the underlying chemical elements. Our attention focuses on sophisticated machines and circuits, not the minerals that make their manufacture possible. Yet behind this technological sophistication lies a group of materials whose importance has grown exponentially in recent decades: rare earths.

Despite a name unfamiliar to the general public, these elements are ubiquitous in vital industrial applications. They are fundamental to the manufacture of high-performance permanent magnets, automotive catalysts, optical materials, sensors, lasers, and advanced electronic devices. Their relevance therefore extends beyond primary mineral extraction, reaching strategic sectors such as energy, urban mobility, medicine, and telecommunications.

This global interest accompanies a structural economic transformation. As nations expand investment in electrification, renewable energy, and digital technologies, demand surges for components delivering high performance in compact, efficient equipment. In this context, rare earths have assumed an indispensable strategic position. According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), demand for the four elements most closely associated with high-performance magnets has doubled since 2015. Projections based on current policies indicate that this consumption could grow by approximately another third by 2030 [1].

It is crucial, however, to avoid technological determinism. Not all modern solutions necessarily depend on these elements; engineering architectures, including particular motors and generators, can operate without rare-earth magnets. Nevertheless, certain intrinsic magnetic and optical properties make these materials irreplaceable in cutting-edge applications, explaining their central role in the energy transition.

## Chemical definition and the paradox of abundance

To understand these materials' industrial and geopolitical influence, we must clarify their chemical identity. “Rare earths” does not designate a single mineral but a group of 17 chemical elements: the 15 lanthanides (from lanthanum, atomic number 57, to lutetium, atomic number 71), plus scandium and yttrium, included because of their analogous chemical properties [2].

The term suggests extreme scarcity, but its origin is historical. In early chemistry, “earth” identified insoluble mineral oxides, and “rare” reflected the difficulty of isolating these substances. Subsequent research demonstrated that several of these elements are relatively abundant in Earth's crust; cerium, for example, is more abundant than many common industrial metals.

There is, however, a fundamental distinction between geological abundance and economic availability. An element may be diffusely distributed through enormous volumes of rock without forming concentrations (grades) that justify a mining operation. Even at attractive grades, its recovery often depends on complex and economically costly chemical processes. The difficulty therefore lies not necessarily in geological discovery, but in identifying workable deposits and mastering the technology needed to turn them into industrial products. This distinction explains why the term “rare” persists despite the crustal abundance of some members of the group.

## Technological diversity and specific applications

Rare earths are not a monolithic bloc; each element exhibits different technological behavior. While neodymium and praseodymium are valued by the permanent-magnet industry, others, such as europium and terbium, are essential for light emission and luminescence, and cerium and lanthanum are crucial for chemical catalysis and precision polishing [2–4].

Consequently, substantial mineral reserves do not guarantee abundant supplies of every sought-after element. A deposit may be rich in cerium and lanthanum (light rare earths) yet poor in dysprosium and terbium (heavy rare earths), which are geologically rarer and more technologically critical for high-temperature applications. Different geological formations require specific beneficiation and separation techniques. Any deposit assessment must therefore consider not only total tonnage, but also detailed chemical composition, mineralogy, and the metallurgical conditions governing recovery.

## Materials science and atomic distinctiveness

The technological value of rare earths derives directly from their atomic structure. Lanthanides have electronic configurations characterized by the progressive filling of 4f orbitals, which are protected (“shielded”) by the outer 5s and 5p orbitals. This distinctive characteristic gives them exceptional magnetic, optical, and spectroscopic properties that are difficult to reproduce with more common elements.

In permanent-magnet manufacture, chemical composition and microstructure directly influence magnetic-field strength and coercivity—the ability to resist demagnetization under opposing fields or elevated temperatures. This resistance is vital for equipment operating under extreme conditions. Specific optical properties also enable applications in lasers and precision instrumentation. Value therefore comes not merely from occurrence in nature, but from the controlled use of these physical properties in materials engineered through materials science. Knowing the composition is only the beginning; mastering its transformation into a functional component is the real technological challenge.

## Strategic applications: motors, wind power, and the battery myth

Neodymium–iron–boron (NdFeB) permanent magnets represent the most strategic application. They combine high magnetic strength with compact dimensions and are essential in high-efficiency electric motors, wind generators, and industrial sensors. The characteristic magnetic phase is based on neodymium, iron, and boron; praseodymium (Pr) can replace some neodymium (Nd), while dysprosium (Dy) and terbium (Tb) may be added according to performance and temperature requirements to improve coercivity and thermal performance. According to the IEA, permanent magnets account for around 95% of the value of rare-earth consumption, although they do not represent the same proportion by mass [1]. This illustrates how small quantities of material can add enormous economic value when incorporated into high-technology components.

In electric vehicles, permanent-magnet motors offer advantages in power density and efficiency, crucial for range and space. However, not all electric cars use them; induction motors do without rare earths and provide a valid alternative depending on the manufacturer's strategy and input availability [4]. In wind power, direct-drive turbines benefit enormously from permanent-magnet generators, but other electromagnetic configurations exist. The relationship between rare earths and renewable energy is therefore one of technological advantage, rather than absolute universal dependence.

It is imperative to dispel the frequent confusion with batteries. Lithium, nickel, cobalt, and graphite belong to different battery supply chains, depending on the chemistry used. Rare earths, by contrast, are primarily relevant to energy conversion (motors/generators). Although some nickel–metal hydride (Ni-MH) batteries use rare earths, the lithium-ion batteries predominant today do not require them as a mandatory component. Investing in battery factories therefore does not resolve dependence on permanent magnets; these are distinct production chains with different material requirements.

## Economic geology: minerals, deposits, and viability

Geologically, rare earths occur in minerals such as monazite (a potentially radioactive phosphate), bastnäsite (a fluorocarbonate and major industrial source), and xenotime (a phosphate rich in yttrium and heavy rare earths). In addition, ion-adsorption clay deposits, formed by intense weathering, are strategically important because they concentrate heavy rare earths adsorbed onto clay surfaces. In Brazil, favorable environments include alkaline/carbonatite complexes (Minas Gerais), granites/pegmatites (Pitinga, Amazonas), and ion-adsorption clays (Minaçu, Goiás) [5].

However, discovering rare earths is not equivalent to discovering a viable mine. Before any operation, rigorous geological studies, drilling, mineralogical characterization, and beneficiation tests are required. Economic viability depends on infrastructure, operating costs, price volatility, and environmental licensing. Two occurrences generically classified as “rare-earth deposits” can present entirely different industrial challenges because of variations in composition and mineralogy.

## The processing bottleneck and global asymmetry

Even with a viable deposit, the path to a permanent magnet is long and complex. After extraction and beneficiation to obtain a concentrate, the critical hydrometallurgical separation stage begins. Because lanthanides are chemically similar, separating them requires exhaustive sequences of solvent extraction, consuming energy and reagents. The purified oxides are then reduced to metals and transformed into alloys, undergoing sintering and heat treatments for microstructural control.

Holding reserves does not mean mastering manufacturing. The international distribution of the production chain reveals this asymmetry: in 2024, for magnet rare earths (neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium, and terbium), China accounted for ~60% of mining and 91% of refining; its share of sintered permanent-magnet manufacture reached 94% [1]. Industrial dominance lies in processing and manufacturing capability, not simply in possessing raw ore.

## Reserves versus production: the Brazilian case

Economic geology distinguishes a “resource” (a concentration with prospects for recovery) from a “reserve” (the demonstrated economically mineable portion). The original edition uses an estimate of approximately 21 million tonnes of Brazilian reserves (REO), attributed to the USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026, placing Brazil second globally behind China (~44 Mt) [3].

Nevertheless, geological potential contrasts with production realities. For 2025, the USGS estimated world production at ~390 thousand tonnes (REO), with China accounting for ~270 thousand t and Brazil for ~2 thousand t [3]. An important methodological caveat: Brazil's National Mining Agency (ANM) reported Brazilian production of 326 tonnes of oxide concentrate in 2025 [6]. This discrepancy reflects methodological differences and product definitions between agencies, not necessarily an error. The ANM figure is monitored and specific to declared concentrates; the USGS estimate may include extrapolations or different calculation bases. Whatever the precise metric, Brazilian production is marginal relative to its potential and global demand.

Exploration interest is growing, however: mineral exploration permits for rare earths increased by 485.4% between 2021 and 2025 [6]. This upturn is positive, but mining titles do not automatically turn into production; they depend on geological results, technical and economic viability, and licensing. It is vital to distinguish operating sites, exploration projects, and mere occurrences, using official sources such as the Geological Survey of Brazil (SGB) [7, 8] to map national potential realistically.

## Sustainability, recycling, and the materials transition

The importance of rare earths to low-carbon technologies does not eliminate their environmental impacts. Extraction and processing involve soil removal, water consumption, effluent generation, and tailings management, as well as radiological risks in minerals such as monazite. Recent scientific reviews highlight risks of water contamination and soil alteration, requiring integrated sustainable management [9, 10]. Ion-adsorption clay deposits demand particular attention to in-situ leaching and aquifer protection. Sustainability must be demonstrated operationally, not merely asserted commercially.

Emerging alternatives include recycling magnets from discarded equipment and bioleaching to recover materials from waste [11]. However, recycling still faces barriers of scale and logistics and cannot immediately replace primary production as demand grows [12]. Supply-chain traceability is essential for transparency and risk management [13].

The energy transition is inherently a materials transition. Replacing fossil fuels with electrification changes mineral demand: copper for grids, lithium for batteries, silicon for photovoltaics, and rare earths for magnetism/optics. Policies focused solely on extraction overlook decisive refining and manufacturing stages. Expanding mining without developing processing capabilities does not guarantee industrial diversification or supply security [1, 14].

## Conclusion: the paradox of wealth and the technological bottleneck

Concluding this first part of the series requires confronting an uncomfortable yet fundamental paradox: having ore underground is merely the prelude to a monumental industrial challenge. The simplistic narrative that “holding reserves guarantees development” constitutes a geological fallacy that ignores the complexity of materials science and metallurgical engineering. As extensively demonstrated, the crustal abundance of the 17 rare-earth elements does not automatically translate into economic availability. The real added value lies not in raw rock, but in the technological capacity to unravel the electronic configurations of 4f orbitals and transform them, with atomic precision, into high-performance components such as neodymium–iron–boron permanent magnets.

When we analyze Brazil's situation with the analytical rigor the subject demands, the asymmetry becomes striking. Brazil boasts the planet's second-largest estimated reserves, at around 21 million tonnes. Yet our actual annual concentrate production ranges from a mere 326 tonnes (ANM administrative data) to 2,000 tonnes (USGS estimates). This gulf between geological potential and production realities exposes a severe structural vulnerability: without mastering the critical stages of refining, hydrometallurgical separation, and alloy manufacture, the country faces the historical risk of perpetuating an archaic extractive model, exporting raw materials at negligible prices and reimporting finished technology at exorbitant costs. The substantial 485.4% increase in mineral exploration permits is a positive sign of growing activity, but mining titles do not translate into industrial sovereignty without massive investment in research and development, logistics infrastructure, and the training of highly specialized human capital.

Furthermore, the environmental dimension cannot be treated as a mere bureaucratic appendix or an obstacle to circumvent, but as a central pillar of any project's viability. Separating chemically similar elements requires energy- and reagent-intensive processes, creating real risks of water contamination and complex tailings-management challenges, especially in ion-adsorption clay deposits or thorium-bearing minerals. Sustainability must therefore be demonstrated through auditable operational data and supply-chain traceability, never through “green marketing” rhetoric.

Ultimately, the global energy transition is undeniably a materials transition. But the most critical lesson at this stage is that geology merely deals the cards; those who master the value chain set the rules of the game. Ore itself is silent. Technology gives it a voice and value.

This inevitably leads us to the next frontier of the discussion. If holding reserves does not guarantee power, who actually controls the refining and manufacture of the components that move the world? How did China consolidate near-absolute dominance, and what strategies are global powers pursuing to mitigate this dependence? In Part 2 of this series, we will leave the laboratory and mine to enter the geopolitical arena of rare earths, where the struggle over technology's future begins long before the first atom is extracted.
